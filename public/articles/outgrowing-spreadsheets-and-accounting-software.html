Seven signs you have outgrown spreadsheets and accounting software

Operations · Published November 11, 2026 · By the Nastaliq team

The short answer

You have outgrown spreadsheets when the same number lives in three places, month-end takes longer every quarter and only one person understands the workbook. Move inventory and purchasing into one system with accounting first, then sales.

Spreadsheets and an entry-level accounting package are the right tools for a young company. They are cheap and everyone knows them. The trouble starts quietly, somewhere after the first few million in revenue, when the business has more moving parts than the tools were built for. These are the signs.

1. The same data is typed more than once

An order is written in an email, entered on a sheet, then keyed into the accounting package as an invoice. Each step is a chance for a wrong quantity or price, and each one costs minutes that add up to days.

2. Nobody trusts the stock number

Sales asks the warehouse before promising anything, because the sheet is usually wrong. Staff walk the shelves to find out what to buy. When the record and the shelf disagree, people stop using the record.

3. Month-end takes longer every quarter

Closing the books means collecting exports, matching them by hand and chasing differences. If the close has grown from days to weeks, the tools are no longer keeping up with the volume.

4. One person holds the workbook together

There is a master spreadsheet with formulas only its author understands. When that person is on holiday, questions wait. When they leave, the company loses part of its memory.

5. Simple questions take days

What is our margin on this customer? What is on order and when does it land? Which bills are overdue? If each answer needs someone to build a report, decisions are being made late or on instinct.

6. Adding people means sharing logins

Per-user pricing pushes teams to share accounts, so nobody can tell who changed what. A system that charges per seat discourages exactly the people on the floor who should be recording the work.

7. Customers notice

A wrong price on an invoice, a short shipment or a late order all trace back to information that did not reach the right person in time. By the time customers see the cracks, the team has been working around them for months.

What changes with one system

In an ERP, a purchase order, the goods received, the supplier bill and the accounting entry are one chain. A sale reduces stock and posts to the books without anyone re-keying it. Each role sees the same live numbers.

What to move first

  1. Inventory and purchasing, because that is where most double entry and most drift come from.
  2. Accounting, so stock movements and bills post to the books as they happen.
  3. Sales, so orders and customer prices use the same records.

Prepare before you switch

Clean your item list, agree on units and costs, tidy the customer and supplier lists, and ask your accountant for opening balances. A system loaded with clean data on day one earns the team's trust quickly. You do not need everything perfect: start with the records you use every day.

Common questions

When should a company move to an ERP?

When the cost of keeping separate tools in step is visible: data typed more than once, stock figures nobody trusts, a slow month-end and decisions waiting on someone to build a report. Revenue is a rough guide, and the signs matter more than the number.

Can we keep our accounting software and add separate inventory tools?

You can, but every link between tools is something to maintain, and the numbers drift whenever a sync fails or someone skips a step. One system removes the links.

What data do we need ready before moving to an ERP?

A clean item list with units and costs, your customer and supplier lists, open orders and bills, stock on hand by location, and opening balances from your accountant.
